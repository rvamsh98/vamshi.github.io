“CONFIRM TERMINATION”: Anatomy of an O365 Credential-Harvesting Phish Sent From Inside the Tenant

Date: June 2026  ·  Read time: ~6 min

phishingemail forensicsincident responseMITRE ATT&CKMicrosoft 365

TL;DR

A “CONFIRM TERMINATION” email targeting University of New Haven Office 365 users turned out to be a credential-harvesting campaign. The twist: Exchange authentication headers showed the message was submitted as internal — it likely came from a compromised account inside the tenant, not a spoofed external sender. The embedded link led to a Google Form impersonating the IT Help Desk, collecting O365 credentials and personal information. Mapped to MITRE ATT&CK T1566.001 (Spearphishing Link), confirmed malicious by UNH IT, and purged from affected mailboxes.

1. The lure

The email arrived with the subject line CONFIRM TERMINATION. The premise was simple and effective: your Office 365 account is about to be terminated unless you verify it immediately. One link. One threat. One impulsive click is all it takes. This is textbook fear-based social engineering — manufacture urgency, short-circuit critical thinking, and route the victim to a credential-collection page before skepticism kicks in.

2. First look: phishing indicators

Even before touching the headers, the message showed classic signs: fear and urgency-based language; threat of account closure; generic messaging with no personalization; IT support impersonation; and a suspicious shortened hyperlink (forms.gle) hiding the real destination. Any one of these is worth a second look. Together, they are a strong phishing signal.

3. Under the hood: email header forensics

This is where the investigation got interesting. The authentication results were thin — DKIM: None, DMARC: None. But the Exchange headers told a much bigger story: X-MS-Exchange-Organization-AuthAs: Internal, and X-MS-Exchange-CrossTenant-AuthAs: Internal.

What this means: the message was submitted by an authenticated session inside the Microsoft 365 tenant — not relayed in from outside. Combined with the phishing content and harvesting behavior, the assessed root cause is that the sending account itself was compromised and then used as a distribution vector for the campaign.

Why it matters: internally-authenticated mail typically skips the perimeter email-security stack, and recipients inherently trust messages that appear to come from inside their own organization. A compromised internal sender is one of the hardest phishing scenarios to catch — and one of the most effective.

4. Following the link

The embedded hyperlink was a shortened URL: hxxps://forms[.]gle/16PMRGhmVbrD12ZR9 — expanding it revealed the true destination: hxxps://docs[.]google[.]com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSfNdIi6Wv_cJIpOYnTE27dZ7PZsm7sI8pE516FA4_8FDeLENw/viewform. URLScan.io analysis showed the destination hosted on Google infrastructure with a valid TLS certificate and legitimate domain reputation — no infrastructure-based indicators of compromise. This is trusted-platform abuse in its purest form: nothing about the infrastructure is malicious — the content is. Reputation-based detection sees a clean Google domain and waves it through.

5. The harvest: landing page analysis

The destination was a Google Form impersonating an IT Help Desk portal. It requested: Office 365 school email; Password (PWD); full name; phone number; previous university/college attended; previous university email address.

The standout finding: the mismatch between the O365-verification theme and the Google Forms destination. A legitimate IT department does not verify Microsoft 365 accounts through a Google Form. That discrepancy alone is a high-confidence malicious-intent indicator. The extra PII fields suggest the operators were building profiles for follow-on targeting — credential harvesting as the primary objective, information gathering as the secondary.

7. MITRE ATT&CK mapping

Tactic: Initial Access. Technique: Spearphishing Link — T1566.001. Assessed adversary objectives, in order: credential harvesting, then account compromise, then information gathering — with the harvested O365 credentials positioned for follow-on access.

8. Containment and remediation (NIST SP 800-61)

The University of New Haven IT department confirmed the message as malicious and removed it from all affected mailboxes. Recommended follow-through: (1) reset credentials and revoke active sessions for the compromised sending account and anyone who submitted data to the form; (2) block the identified URLs and domains; (3) investigate the sender account’s activity and review authentication logs for lateral abuse; (4) re-enable affected accounts under MFA and restore normal mail flow; (5) monitor for recurrence of the same lure or infrastructure.

9. Lessons learned

Trusted domains do not guarantee trusted content — the most dangerous phish here came from inside the tenant. Legitimate cloud platforms get abused — Google Forms in this instance — and reputation-based controls are blind to it. Compromised internal accounts bypass perimeter filtering and inherit the organization’s trust; internal-sender anomaly detection is not optional. URL expansion and landing-page analysis are non-negotiable steps — the shortened link hid everything. Defense in depth for this scenario: org-wide MFA enforcement, DKIM/DMARC enforcement, detection for anomalous internal-sender behavior, and targeted user awareness training on exactly this pattern.
